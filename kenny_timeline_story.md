# The Making of Kenny — A Timeline

---

## Chapter 1: The Spark (2000–2006)

Before there was a computer, there was a phone.

In 2005, the Vemagiri household got its first mobile phone — a Nokia N70. It was his father's, but to a young Kenny, it was more than a phone. It was a miniature computer, and in many ways, it felt smarter than the actual computers he would soon encounter.

2006 marked two firsts: the first year of formal schooling, and the first computer lesson. The machines ran Windows XP. The first application he ever opened? Microsoft Paint.

The classroom had a system — two students per computer. One sat directly in front of the screen, the other squeezed in beside them. Your turn at the center seat depended on whether your roll number was odd or even, alternating each session. Kenny learned patience before he learned programming.

That was the first love affair with computers. It would not be the last.

---

## Chapter 2: The Hunger (2007–2009)

By 2007, the curriculum expanded — MS Office, Word documents, the building blocks of digital literacy. But outside the classroom, something else was accelerating. India's mobile phone revolution was in full swing, and like most kids his age, Kenny's attention drifted toward mobile gaming.

Yet underneath the games, a quiet ambition was forming: *to have a computer at home.*

It would take years. In the meantime, spotting a desktop at a relative's house felt like finding treasure.

Then came 2009, and with it — the internet.

Kenny's first searches were beautifully simple: Google Images of Indian film heroes. Each image took 30 to 40 seconds to load, pixel by pixel, line by line. He would guess who the actor was before the picture fully rendered. Downloading a movie poster felt like a thrill.

The internet was slow. The curiosity was not.

---

## Chapter 3: The Pirate & The Builder (2010–2012)

By 2010, Kenny had discovered torrents.

It was, by all technical definitions, illegal. But to a ten-year-old in India with limited access to content, it was a revelation. Movies would download overnight — the PC humming through the dark while the house slept. If anyone asked why the computer was still on, he couldn't exactly explain what "seeding" meant.

The PC, technically, belonged to his sister. She was studying Electronics and Communication Engineering at the time.

Then came 2011, and a change of school changed everything.

In a computer science class, Kenny saw his first `.html` file. The moment the code rendered into a webpage, the mystery of the internet shattered in the best possible way. Until that point, websites existed in a kind of Neverland — magical, untouchable, built by someone else. That single HTML file made it real. *People build these. I could build these.*

2012 was the year of deep research. Operating systems. PC vs Mac. The history of Silicon Valley. The dot-com crash. He consumed it all — not for a grade, not for a project, but because he needed to know.

By the end of that year, the decision was made:

> *"Computers are my life. This is me."*

There was just one problem. To pursue computer science formally, he needed mathematics. Kenny had been a topper in science — his parents were both teachers (science and social studies). But maths? That was a different beast. He chose it anyway.

---

## Chapter 4: The Grind (2013–2015)

2013 through 2015 were the quiet years — the ones that don't make for dramatic storytelling, but without them, nothing that followed would have been possible.

Kenny attacked mathematics with a seriousness that surprised even himself. He had never cared this much about a subject he didn't naturally love. But it was the toll booth on the road to where he wanted to be, so he paid it.

He finished schooling in 2014 and entered his 12th standard with maths as his chosen weapon.

There was an irony during this period that would only become clear later: Kenny understood that applications and software were *programmed*, but he didn't know that programming involved *languages*. The concept of a "programming language" — something you learn, with syntax and grammar and rules — simply hadn't crossed his path yet.

He was also, by this point, completely cut off from technology. No computer at home. No mobile phone — he wasn't yet an adult, and the college hostel had a strict no-phones policy.

The boy who fell in love with computers at age six was now, at fifteen, studying mathematics in a hostel with no screen in sight.

---

## Chapter 5: The Revolution (2016)

The isolation stretched into the first half of 2016. Then India changed overnight.

Reliance Jio launched its 4G network with an offer that sounded too good to be true: free SIM cards, free 4G data — *unlimited* data. No calculations, no rationing, no watching the meter. Just... unlimited.

This was the moment India's internet went from a luxury to a utility. Millions came online for the first time. The digital divide didn't close — it exploded open.

Kenny almost missed it entirely. Locked in a college hostel with no phone and no internet, the biggest technological shift of his generation was happening just beyond the walls.

Almost.
